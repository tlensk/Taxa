### <p align="center">Classification in Complex Systems: from Art to Algorithm.</p>
### <p align="center">A Comparative View on Plant Diseases, Medical Diagnostics, and Phage Taxonomy</p>

<p align="center">Tatiana Lenskaia<sup>1,*</sup> and Sergei Lenskii<sup>1</sup></p>
<p align="center"><sup>1</sup> Department of Molecular Genetics, Temerty School of Medicine, University of Toronto</p>
<p align="center"><sup>*</sup> Corresponding author: t.lenskaia@utoronto.ca</p>

#### Abstract
<p>Classification is a foundational act in science, yet its meaning and method shift dramatically as systems grow in complexity. This paper traces the evolution of diagnostic reasoning - from its practical origins in plant pathology to its institutional forms in medicine and its computational implementation in modern viral taxonomy - to illustrate how classification in complex systems moves from art toward automation. Plant disease diagnostics reveal the epistemological challenges inherent in systems where symptoms are ambiguous, causal agents are diverse, and both environmental and genetic contexts shape outcomes. These challenges highlight deeper ontological questions about what counts as a “disease,” a “pathogen,” or even an “error” in diagnosis, and how such categories depend on imperfect evidence and domain-specific constraints. Medicine builds on similar principles but with greater formalization, standardized classification systems, and a stronger commitment to probabilistic integration of heterogeneous evidence. Yet both domains confront the same fundamental problem: in complex, nonlinear, context-dependent systems, no single feature can reliably define identity. This insight extends naturally to the classification of tailed bacteriophages, themselves pathogens of pathogenic bacteria. Their mosaic genomes, modular evolution, and structural diversity mirror the uncertainties faced in plant and medical diagnostics. Contemporary phage taxonomy, particularly approaches built on curated Hidden Markov Models for morphogenetic proteins, exemplifies a shift toward computational diagnostics that model identity as an inference over weighted features rather than as a static morphological label. By comparing these three domains, this paper argues that diagnostic reasoning provides a unifying framework for understanding and classifying complex biological systems, and that the future of taxonomy lies not in replacing an expert with an automate, but in incorporating diagnostic reasoning into probabilistic, interpretable computational models.
</p>

#### Keywords
<p>Complex systems; classification; diagnostic reasoning; plant pathology; medical diagnostics; viral taxonomy; bacteriophages.
</p>

<p>Diagnostic systems sit at the uneasy intersection of what diseases are and how we come to know them. Their development reflects ongoing attempts to reconcile the ontological complexity of biological phenomena - the inherently multicausal, context-dependent nature of disease - with the epistemological constraints of observation, inference, and classification. Nowhere is this tension more visible than in plant pathology, where diagnosticians must interpret symptoms that emerge from layered interactions among host genetics, physiology, microbe communities, agricultural practices, and environmental fluctuations. Unlike the structured diagnostic frameworks available in medicine, plant diagnostic work often occurs under conditions of uncertainty, with limited standardized algorithms and minimal opportunities for systematic post hoc assessment. The very notion of “disease” in plants is ontologically diffuse: symptoms may arise without a pathogen, pathogens may remain latent without symptoms, and environmental factors may modulate both in nonlinear ways. As a result, diagnostic outcomes frequently hinge on epistemological choices - what to sample, what tests to run, which causal narratives are considered plausible.</p>
<p>Our experiences with diagnosing plant pathology cases illustrate how an overly reductionist view obscures the true structure of disease. In practice, cases often begin with the assumption that a single, identifiable causal agent must be responsible for observed symptoms. Yet plant-specific variability - rooted in genotype, development, microenvironment, and historical exposure - can dramatically alter disease manifestation (Lenskaia et al., 2022). The classical Paracelsian insight that “the dose makes the poison” applies as much to plant physiology as to toxicology: thresholds for infection, damage, and recovery vary across individuals and environments. In many diagnostic submissions, viral testing becomes a default “last resort” only after fungi, bacteria, and abiotic causes have been eliminated, reflecting an implicit hierarchy of causation rather than a systems perspective that recognizes disease as emergent. But symptoms often mislead. As we saw in the case of soybean mottling (Lenskaia et al., 2023), examining only symptomatic leaves delayed diagnosis; the true causal processes were spatially and temporally decoupled from where symptoms appeared. This discrepancy between where disease is visible and where causation operates is not an exception - it is a structural feature of biological complexity. The ontological lesson is that symptoms are surface-level expressions of deeper, distributed processes. The epistemological lesson is that inference cannot rely on first impressions.</p>
<p>The maize case (Lenskaia et al., 2024) that took more than two decades to resolve reveals an even more profound misalignment between diagnostic expectations and biological reality. All routine pathogen tests failed, yet the system itself resisted interpretation using pathogen-focused frameworks. Only with the maturation of genetic engineering, comparative genomics, and a reconsideration of plausible ontologies did the true cause emerge: a genetically encoded predisposition linked to the <i>teosinte branched1 (tb1)</i> locus that interacted with resource limitations to produce disease-like outcomes. The maize was not “infected” but genetically primed for excessive tillering under specific environmental conditions. This case illustrates how diagnostics depend not only on available tools but on the conceptual space in which explanations are sought. When the ontology of disease is too narrowly defined - restricted to infectious agents - epistemology becomes constrained, and diagnostic puzzles remain unsolved. Methodological innovation alone is insufficient; new conceptual categories must evolve in parallel.</p>
<p>These challenges in plant pathology resonate with, but also diverge from, the better-institutionalized landscape of medical diagnostics. Medicine has developed robust classification frameworks, such as the International Classification of Diseases [https://icd.who.int/en/], supported by extensive clinical trial infrastructures, standardized algorithms, and postmortem validation. Physicians are trained not merely to detect pathogens but to evaluate host-specific factors, comorbidities, and genetic predispositions. Yet even in medicine, diagnostic thresholds can be arbitrary. They represent not purely biological constants but pragmatic, value-dependent choices. These thresholds are set by guideline panels, influenced by trade-offs between sensitivity and specificity, and they can shift as risk-benefit judgments change - as seen in blood pressure (McGrath & McEvoy, 2020), diabetes (Florkowski, 2013), PSA (Ilic et al., 2018), and BMI (Flegal, 2023)  thresholds. Decision-theory and diagnostic-methods literature formalize how thresholds should be chosen (based on priors and utilities) but also document that many real-world cutoffs are pragmatic and contestable (Steinhauser et al., 2016).</p> 
<p>In addition, cross-reactive assays produce misleading positives. Immunoassays can yield misleading positive results when antibodies or antigens cross-react with unintended targets, and medical literature provides clear evidence of this phenomenon. For example, cardiac troponin assays have been shown to give false-positive elevations attributable to heterophile antibodies, non-specific human antibodies that bind assay reagents and create spurious signals (Chaulin, 2022). In another domain, serologic assays for flaviviruses such as dengue and Zika repeatedly demonstrate antigenic cross-reactivity due the shared antigenic epitopes between these viruses (Sharp et al., 2019), and serology alone can misidentify which flavivirus caused infection without neutralization tests or molecular confirmation.</p>
<p>Symptom-based reasoning can fail in conditions that are systemic, latent, or genetically modulated. For example, systematic reviews of diagnostic difficulty in primary care (Kostopoulou et al., 2008; Vally et al., 2023) found that diseases which are atypical in presentation, non-specific in symptoms, of low prevalence, or masked by comorbidity are more likely to be delayed or misdiagnosed. Also, the literature on “medically unexplained symptoms” (Jutel, 2010; Rosendal et al., 2017) shows that patients with persistent symptoms across multiple body systems often resist classification by standard symptom‐based models, since the underlying processes may involve central sensitisation, functional disorders or latent conditions rather than a straightforward disease entity. These findings underscore that relying solely on observable symptoms without integrating broader picture including genetic and systemic factors can lead to diagnostic blind spots, especially when disease processes are latent, multisystemic, or modulated by individual characteristics of patients.</p> 
<p>Thus, medicine also confronts the ontological complexity - where multiple causes act simultaneously - and the epistemological limitations - where tests are imperfect and categories provisional. The difference lies in scale: medicine has built institutional mechanisms to continually recalibrate its epistemology; plant pathology has not. As a result, plant disease diagnostics still operate closer to detective work than algorithmic decision-making. The consequences of diagnostic errors, though often invisible to diagnosticians, can cascade through agricultural systems and global supply chains, highlighting the need for stronger conceptual and methodological foundations.</p>
<p>These insights set the stage for re-examining the basis for viral taxonomy [https://ictv.global/taxonomy] - particularly the classification of tailed bacteriophages (Ackermann, 1998) - as an example of how biology reorganizes its conceptual frameworks in response to complexity. Phages are viruses that prey on prokaryotes, they are pathogens of pathogens, shaping the ecology, evolution, and virulence of microorganisms that cause diseases in plants, animals, and humans. Understanding phages therefore requires acknowledging two layers of complexity: that of viruses themselves, and that of the microbial communities they modulate. Historically, phage classification relied on morphological hierarchies rooted in tail structure, embedding an implicit assumption that similarity reflects shared ancestry. But as comparative genomics expanded, ontological cracks appeared. Tailed phage genomes exhibit mosaic architectures (Casjens, 2005; Hatfull, 2008), extensive horizontal gene transfer (Touchon et al., 2017), and network-like evolutionary relationships (Lima-Mendez et al., 2008) that defy simple lineage boundaries. The traditional hierarchical ontology - assigning viruses to discrete families based on a shared structural template - could not accommodate these realities (Simmonds et al., 2017). The epistemological tools of the time limited what could be known.</p>
<p>The genomic era forced a shift toward continuum-based frameworks (Turner et al., 2021), gene-sharing networks (Bolduc et al., 2017), and comprehensive phylogenetic analyses (Low et al., 2019)  that acknowledge the distributed nature of phage identity. This transition mirrors the shift required in plant pathology: moving from discrete, pathogen-centered views of disease toward systems-based ontologies that represent biological complexity more faithfully. In both fields, the evolution of classification is not merely a technical adjustment but a reorientation of what counts as a meaningful unit of analysis. For phages, this means understanding them as dynamic participants in microbial ecosystems, whose identities and functional roles emerge from interactions rather than fixed categories. For plant pathology, it means recognizing that disease outcomes are shaped by multilayer interactions among hosts, pathogens, environments, and human practices.</p>
<p>Therefore, diagnostics - whether classifying phages, identifying plant pathogens, or diagnosing human disease - are not neutral acts of detection. They are expressions of our underlying ontological commitments and epistemological strategies. When those commitments assume simplicity, diagnostics falter. When they embrace complexity - acknowledging nonlinearity, heterogeneity, indirectness, and historical contingency - they become powerful tools for navigating the biological world. The intertwined lessons from plant pathology, medicine, and phage biology suggest that the future of diagnostics lies not in perfecting isolated diagnostic methods but in refining the conceptual frameworks that make diagnostic reasoning viable.</p>
<p>In conclusion, the progression of classification from art to algorithm reflects the deepening epistemological and ontological understanding of the systems we study. In plant pathology, early diagnostic practice resembled an interpretive craft - an art guided by tacit knowledge, contextual inference, and accumulated experience - because the underlying biological systems were too complex, too poorly understood, or too poorly measured to permit rigid criteria. Medicine followed a similar trajectory: as latent, systemic, or genetically modulated diseases revealed the limits of symptom-based reasoning, the field moved toward formalized and evidence-based methods, molecular assays, and probabilistic decision frameworks. Even in phage taxonomy - where the “patients” are themselves pathogens of plants, animals, and humans - the shift from morphology-based groupings to genome-anchored, algorithmic classification became possible only once the complexity of viral evolution was sufficiently mapped to allow hierarchy to be imposed. Seen across these domains, classification becomes algorithmic not simply because computation is available, but because mechanistic insight accumulates to the point where once-entangled variables can be discerned, defined, and reordered into reproducible criteria. The art is never fully lost - it persists in uncertainty, exceptions, and boundary cases - but as our models of biological complexity mature, classifications become increasingly scalable, transforming fragmented local practices into global, systematic frameworks.</p>
